Backup hours roughly equal battery volts times amp-hours times about 0.6 divided by your load in watts, so a 1500VA inverter with two 150Ah batteries runs a 500 watt load for around 4 hours. The 0.6 factor combines the usable depth of discharge and inverter losses, and it keeps your estimate honest.

This guide is researched from standard electrical arithmetic and typical Indian lead-acid battery behaviour, not from measuring your home. Before calculating, collect the real wattage of each appliance from its label, because listed ratings are often higher than what a device draws in normal use.

## Step 1: convert VA to watts

VA is apparent power; watts are the real power your appliances use. The conversion depends on power factor. Inverters are commonly around 0.8, so a 1500VA inverter delivers roughly 1,200 watts at best. UPS units for computers are often around 0.6 to 0.7, so a 1 kVA UPS gives roughly 600-700 watts.

Never load a UPS or inverter to its limit. Plan to keep the load at 70-80 percent of the watt rating so that the unit stays cool and efficient.

## Step 2: add up your load

Rough figures: a ceiling fan at 70-80 watts, an LED bulb at 9-12 watts, a TV at 80-150 watts, a Wi-Fi router at 10-20 watts, a laptop at 50-70 watts, a desktop at 150-300 watts, and a refrigerator at 100-200 watts when the compressor is on. Do not include any AC or geyser in a home inverter plan.

## Step 3: calculate battery energy

Multiply volts by amp-hours. A 12V 150Ah battery stores 1,800 Wh. Two in series for 24V give 24 x 150 = 3,600 Wh nominal. Multiplying by 0.6 gives about 2,160 Wh usable and delivered. That 0.6 comes from using only around 70 percent of a lead-acid battery to protect its life and losing around 15 percent in the inverter.

For a lithium battery, usable depth is around 90 percent, so use roughly 0.75 instead.

## Step 4: divide by load

Backup hours = delivered Wh divided by load watts. With 2,160 Wh and a 500 watt load, you get 4.3 hours. At 300 watts it is 7.2 hours, and at 800 watts it is 2.7 hours. Notice that doubling the load more than halves the hours, since high current also reduces effective battery capacity.

## Why real life gives less

Battery age cuts capacity. A three-year-old tubular battery may deliver only 70 to 80 percent of its rated Ah. Temperature matters too: cold reduces capacity, and heat shortens life. Peukert's effect means that high loads draw out less total energy than low loads from the same battery. If you run at near the inverter's limit, expect noticeably shorter runtimes than the formula suggests.

Also, battery Ah ratings are usually quoted at a 20-hour discharge rate. Discharging in four hours gives lower effective capacity.

## Sizing for your cut length

Work backwards. If cuts last 4 hours and your load is 500 watts, you need 2,000 Wh delivered, which means 2,000 divided by 0.6, around 3,300 Wh of battery. That is roughly two 150Ah 12V batteries. For 6-hour cuts at the same load, you need about 5,000 Wh, which means two 200Ah batteries or more.
