## 1. Built-in Gas Hobs

Roughly ₹10,000–40,000. Gas hobs offer instant flame control and work with any cookware, including tawa and direct flame for roasting. The price of that familiarity is more cleaning around burners and extra heat in the kitchen.

## 2. Built-in Induction Hobs

Roughly ₹15,000–60,000. Induction hobs heat the pan directly with a magnetic field, so they are fast and the surface stays cooler. They only work with induction-compatible cookware, so some of your pans may need replacing.

Roughly ₹20,000–50,000. Some hobs combine gas burners with an induction zone, giving both options in one cutout.

## 4. Cookware for Induction

Priced separately from the hob. Induction needs magnetic cookware such as stainless steel with a ferrous base or cast iron. Aluminium and copper without a magnetic base will not work, so budget for replacing a few pans.

## 5. Safety and Heat

Gas hobs have an open flame and need flame failure protection and good ventilation, while induction hobs have cooler surfaces and auto shut-off, which suits homes with children. Each has its own cautions.

## 6. Power Cuts and Load

Induction needs steady electricity and a good electrical connection, since hobs draw high power. Gas works in power cuts but needs a cylinder or piped gas. If your area has frequent outages or voltage swings, that alone can settle the decision.

## How to Choose Between Gas and Induction

Start with your cooking style. If you roast rotis directly on flame or do heavy tadka, gas feels natural, and if you boil, simmer and fry gently, induction works well.

Think about infrastructure. Check your electrical load, the power supply and the gas connection before deciding.

Consider cleaning and safety. Induction has a flat, cool surface that is easy to wipe, while gas has grates and burners to clean.

## Running Costs

Induction is often described as more efficient at transferring heat to the pan, while gas costs depend on cylinder or piped gas prices in your city. Compare your own monthly spend rather than relying on generic figures.

## A Practical Middle Path

If you cannot decide, install a built-in gas hob and keep a portable induction cooktop for boiling and simmering. That lets you test induction cooking at low cost before committing to a built-in induction hob. If you later switch, you will know which cookware works and how it fits your routine.

## Kitchen Planning Notes

Whichever you choose, plan a chimney of suitable suction and a clear cutout. Gas needs a proper connection and ventilation, and induction needs a correctly rated electrical circuit. Check clearances to cabinets and walls, and keep the instruction manual for service.

## Who Should Skip This

If you make fresh rotis over the flame every day, an induction-only hob will frustrate you, because flat magnetic cookware cannot puff a phulka the way direct fire does. Equally, if your wiring is old or shared with several heavy appliances, a built-in induction hob should wait until an electrician has checked the circuit.

## Recommendation

For most Indian kitchens that make rotis daily, a built-in gas hob remains the practical choice, while induction suits those who want speed and easy cleaning. Check the cutout size and your electrical load before you commit to either.
