## What the three names mean

**OLED** panels make light pixel by pixel. A pixel that should be black switches off completely, so contrast is effectively infinite. LG supplies most OLED panels in the market, and Sony, Panasonic and others buy from it. Samsung's QD-OLED is a related design that adds quantum dots to improve colour brightness.

**QLED** is not a different display type. It is an LCD panel lit from behind, with a quantum-dot layer that widens the colour range. The backlight may be cheap edge lighting or a better direct array, which explains why QLED prices vary widely.

**Mini-LED** is also an LCD with a quantum-dot layer, but the backlight uses thousands of tiny LEDs divided into many local dimming zones. More zones means more precise control over which area is bright and which is dark.

## Brightness and bright rooms

This is where Mini-LED pulls ahead. Good Mini-LED sets reach peak highlights in the thousands of nits, so HDR scenes keep their punch even with daylight in the room. OLEDs are usually dimmer on large bright areas, although recent generations have improved. If your hall has large west-facing windows and you watch afternoon cricket, an OLED will look more washed out than a Mini-LED.

Standard QLED sits in the middle. A decent direct-lit QLED looks lovely in daylight, but its blacks turn grey in a dark room because the whole backlight glows.

## Dark rooms and contrast

In a dark room OLED is the clear winner. Starfield scenes, night shots and black bars all look cleaner because nothing glows around bright objects. Mini-LED handles this well but can show a faint halo around subtitles or bright objects on dark backgrounds, depending on zone count. Cheap QLED shows the most grey blacks and uneven corners.

## Burn-in: what is real and what is exaggerated

OLED screens can retain a static image if abused, for example a news channel logo displayed for many hours every day. For mixed viewing of films, streaming and games across a few years, modern OLEDs include pixel shifting and compensation cycles, and most owners never notice it. If you leave a TV on a 24-hour news channel from morning till night, or use it as a computer monitor with a fixed taskbar, buy Mini-LED instead.

Neither QLED nor Mini-LED suffers burn-in in the OLED sense.

## Gaming and sports

For PS5, Xbox or PC gaming, OLED panels respond almost instantly, which makes motion look very clean. Mini-LED sets also support 120Hz and VRR, and their extra brightness helps HDR games. For fast cricket and football, either works; check that motion handling is good rather than relying on a refresh rate number alone. ## Who should skip each one

- **Skip OLED** if your viewing room is bright all day, if you run a single channel for hours, or if you plan on keeping static content on screen.
- **Skip Mini-LED** if you want the absolute cleanest blacks for dark-room movies and do not care about brightness, or if your budget is firmly below ₹60,000.
- **Skip basic QLED** if you watch HDR content seriously. Edge-lit versions do not have the dimming to deliver it, however the box describes them.

## Recommendation

If you only want one TV for the family and the room gets daylight, pick Mini-LED at the size you can afford. If your viewing is mostly night-time films and games, stretch for OLED. Only buy a plain QLED when budget rules, and read the listing for local dimming or direct backlighting rather than trusting the QLED badge alone. For any of the three, check that a service centre exists in your city before paying.
