## Step 1: test signal before you mount

Stand at the door with your phone and run a Wi-Fi speed test or check signal strength. You need a stable connection; a result with around 2 to 3 bars and 5 Mbps upload is a safe minimum for video.

Metal security doors and thick walls block signal. If the test is poor, place a mesh node or router closer first. Do not mount the doorbell until the signal holds.

## Step 2: choose the right height

The common recommendation is around 1.2 to 1.5 metres from the floor. Lower catches parcels and children; higher captures faces at a better angle for adults. Indian doors often have a letterbox or a nameplate at 1.5 metres, so aim for the eye line of an average visitor.

Check the camera's field of view. A wide-angle doorbell captures the doorstep and corridor, but also neighbours' doors, which can create privacy issues in apartment buildings.

## Step 3: position and angle

Mount on the latch side of the door, on the wall or frame, so visitors reach for it naturally. Keep it clear of strong lights behind visitors, which cause silhouettes, and avoid mounting on a surface that vibrates when the door closes.

If the door recesses, a corner or wedge bracket tilts the camera towards the visitor. Walls that angle away often need a wedge. See [doorbell angle mount bracket](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=video+doorbell+angle+mount+bracket&tag=adifystore-21).

## Step 4: pick a power approach

Battery models are the simplest: mount, charge, connect. Wired models need a transformer and correct voltage, and Indian homes rarely have one. A mains-powered bell circuit is not a safe source for a low-voltage doorbell, so call an electrician if you want wired power.

A battery doorbell can also use a solar charger on some models, which suits a balcony door. Check the charging method before you buy. See [solar charger for doorbell](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=solar+panel+for+video+doorbell&tag=adifystore-21).

## Step 5: connect over 2.4 GHz

Most doorbells only join 2.4 GHz networks. Many Indian routers broadcast both bands under one name, and phones then connect the doorbell to 5 GHz, causing failure. Fix: temporarily turn off 5 GHz, or use a separate 2.4 GHz network name, set up the doorbell, then switch it back.

Use a strong password, update firmware after setup, and reserve a fixed IP for the doorbell if your router allows it.

## Step 6: add a chime

If you want to hear the bell anywhere in the flat, add a plug-in wireless chime or use a base station with a speaker. A phone alert is not enough when your phone is in another room. ## Step 7: set motion zones and sensitivity

Out of the box, many doorbells record every passer-by in the corridor. Draw a motion zone limited to the doorstep and reduce sensitivity. This extends battery life and cuts nuisance alerts. Turn on person detection if the model offers it.

## Common mistakes specific to doorbells

- Mounting before testing Wi-Fi, and then fighting disconnections.
- Mounting too high, so you only see the tops of heads.
- Pointing at a bright corridor window, which blanks out faces.
- Using adhesive on dusty or oily surfaces, so the unit falls within weeks.
- Ignoring the chime and missing visitors.

Clean the surface with [isopropyl alcohol wipes](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=isopropyl+alcohol+wipes&tag=adifystore-21) before using adhesive, and use screws on rough walls. ## Privacy and neighbours

In a shared corridor, tilt the camera to your doorstep and mask neighbours' doors in the app if possible. Be considerate about audio recording too; some models can disable it.

## Who should skip DIY installation

If you need to drill into reinforced concrete or a steel door frame, hire a technician. Likewise, a wired installation involving mains should be done by an electrician.

## Recommendation

Test signal first, mount at around 1.2 to 1.5 metres, use a wedge if your door is recessed, connect over 2.4 GHz, and add a chime. If signal is weak, fix the Wi-Fi before the doorbell, and set motion zones to protect battery life and privacy.
