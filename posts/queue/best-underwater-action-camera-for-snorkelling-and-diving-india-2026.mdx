## Start with your real depth, not the spec sheet

Snorkelling at Havelock, Neil Island or Lakshadweep lagoons rarely goes below 3 to 5 metres, and pressure at that depth is trivial. Any camera rated to 10 metres is technically fine. The question is leaks over a long trip, which is why seals matter more than headline numbers.

Scuba is different. Recreational Open Water limits are 18 metres and Advanced goes to 30. A bare camera rated to 10 or 12 metres will flood on a routine dive, so you need a housing rated to at least 40 to 60 metres. Treat the housing as part of the price, usually another ₹4,000-8,000.

## Colour correction: why your reef footage looks blue-green

Water absorbs red light within the first 3 to 5 metres. Without correction, footage turns cyan. There are three practical fixes.

First, use a red filter for blue water (Andaman, Lakshadweep) and a magenta filter for greener water (Goa, Netrani). Filters clip onto the housing and cost a few hundred rupees to a couple of thousand. Second, lock white balance manually instead of leaving it on auto; auto flickers as you change depth. Third, shoot in a flat or log profile if the camera offers it and correct later. See [red dive filters](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=red+dive+filter+action+camera&tag=adifystore-21).

A small video light around 1,000 lumens brings back reds on close subjects like clownfish and corals, and gives sharper footage in the shadows under a ledge.

## Buoyancy and handling

A heavy tray with a big housing can make you a clumsy diver. Aim for a camera that is close to neutral: a foam floating grip for snorkelling, or a small weight or float arm for scuba. Always use a wrist strap or a lanyard clipped to your BCD, because a dropped camera sinks fast and the Andaman floor is not forgiving.

For snorkelling, a floating hand grip is cheap insurance and also keeps your shots steadier. Try [floating camera grips](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=floating+hand+grip+action+camera&tag=adifystore-21).

## Care after every dive

Rinse the whole camera in fresh water for a few minutes, press the buttons while it soaks, then dry before opening the battery door. Check the O-ring for hairs or sand every time, and lightly grease it per the maker's instructions. Silicone grease kits are widely available; a quick look at [O-ring silicone grease](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=silicone+o+ring+grease+camera&tag=adifystore-21) saves a flooded camera.

Keep a spare battery: cool water drains cells quickly, and a typical dive day can burn one battery per two dives in a bare camera.

## Who should skip a dedicated underwater camera

If you only plan one beach holiday with a few minutes of snorkelling, a waterproof pouch for your existing phone may be enough, and certainly cheaper. Divers who want high-end video and stills should also look at a mirrorless camera in a proper housing instead, though that costs many times more.
