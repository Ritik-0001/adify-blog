## How to Choose a Beginner DSLR

Think about lenses. DSLR lenses are plentiful and cheap on the used and new market, which keeps the total cost low.

Check ease of use. A guide mode, clear menus and a comfortable grip help you learn faster.

Consider longevity. DSLRs are in their later years, so if you want to grow into video or newer features, look at entry mirrorless too.

## Learning on a DSLR

Start in aperture priority mode, learn how aperture changes background blur and practise composition in good light. Review your photos on a laptop, not only on the small rear screen, so that you see what actually works. Using only the kit lens for years limits you, and a 50mm prime is the cheapest way to see what the camera can do.

## Buying New, Used or Refurbished

New cameras from authorised sellers come with warranty and support, which matters for a first camera. Used bodies can be good value, but check the shutter count, the sensor for dust, the lens for fungus and the seller's return policy. Refurbished units from the brand can be a middle path, offering some warranty at a lower price.

## Who Should Skip This

If you want to shoot a lot of video, or you expect to keep the camera for ten years and add new lenses, a DSLR will box you in because brands are putting new lenses and features into mirrorless. A casual shooter who mostly takes photos of family at home may also be happier with a good phone and no extra gear to carry.

## Kit Lens, Memory Card and Bag

A beginner kit usually includes only the body and an 18-55mm lens. Budget separately for a fast memory card, a spare battery and a simple padded bag. A cheap card can slow burst shooting, and carrying a bare camera in a backpack invites scratches. These extras cost a small fraction of the camera but decide how smoothly the first months go.

## Recommendation

For most beginners, a Canon EOS 1500D or Nikon D3500 kit is a budget-friendly start, and an entry mirrorless camera is a better long-term bet if you can stretch the budget.
