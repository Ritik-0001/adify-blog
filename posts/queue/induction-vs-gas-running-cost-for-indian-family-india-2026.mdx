## 1. How Efficient Is Each Method

Induction transfers most of its energy into the pan, while a gas flame loses a lot of heat to the air around it. General comparisons often cite induction as noticeably more efficient, but real figures depend on the cookware and cooking style, and you need compatible pans to get the benefit.

## 2. Calculating Induction Cost

Look at the hob's power in kilowatts, estimate your cooking time in hours per month, multiply and apply your electricity tariff. A hob at 2 kilowatts used for an hour at full power draws roughly 2 units, but real use is lower because the power cycles. You will need your own tariff and usage hours for this to mean anything.

## 3. Calculating Gas Cost

Divide the price of a cylinder, or your piped gas bill, by the number of days it lasts. That gives you a daily cost for your actual cooking, which you can compare with the induction estimate. Cylinder prices change, so redo the sum when they do.

## 4. Cookware and Upfront Costs

Induction needs magnetic cookware, which is priced separately, so you may need to replace some pans, while gas works with any pan. Add the hob price, installation and any electrical work to the comparison. Induction usually asks for the larger upfront spend.

## 5. Cooking Style Matters

Direct-flame cooking such as roasting rotis on the flame suits gas, while boiling, simmering and shallow frying suit induction well. Many families use both, with induction for daily tasks and gas for flame work, at the cost of running two systems.

## 6. Power Cuts and Backup

Induction stops during power cuts unless you have an inverter that can handle its load, while gas keeps working. Consider your local reliability before relying on induction alone, and price the backup into your comparison.

## How to Compare for Your Family

Collect your last three months of electricity and gas data. Estimate how many hours you cook per day and which tasks you would move to induction.

Run the numbers for both. Add upfront costs, divide by the monthly saving and see the payback in months, but include comfort, cleaning and safety in your decision.

Think about the long run. Fuel prices change, so a choice that is slightly cheaper today may not stay so.

## A Worked Method You Can Copy

Write down how many hours you cook on each burner per day, the power rating of your induction zone and your electricity rate per unit. Multiply hours by kilowatts and by the rate for a monthly induction estimate. For gas, divide your cylinder price by the days it lasts. Compare the two numbers and add upfront costs, and you will have your own answer.

## Beyond Running Cost

Think about cleaning time, kitchen heat, safety with children and the look of the kitchen. Induction hobs wipe clean easily and keep the room cooler, while gas gives you flame control and works in outages. Sometimes comfort and safety matter more than a small monthly difference.

## Who Should Skip This

If your gas comes from a subsidised cylinder or a piped connection you already pay little for, the running-cost gap may be too small to justify buying a new hob and cookware. The same goes for homes where the wiring is old: spending on electrical upgrades just to switch fuels can wipe out years of savings.

## Recommendation

Do the calculation with your own bills before you decide. If you want efficiency and easy cleaning, a built-in induction hob is a strong option, and if you value flame cooking, keep gas. Whichever you pick, check the electrical load or gas connection with a professional first.
