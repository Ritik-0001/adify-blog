## How to Choose a Camera Gimbal

Start with your camera and lens weight. Add the weight of the body, the lens and accessories like a mic, then choose a gimbal whose payload comfortably exceeds it.

Check compatibility. Brands publish lists of tested camera and lens combinations, which show whether balancing and control cables work.

Think about size and battery. A smaller gimbal travels easily, and a longer battery life helps with long shoots.

## Balancing and Technique

Mount the camera so that its centre of gravity sits on the gimbal's axes, then adjust the tilt, roll and pan arms until the camera stays level when you let go. Newer gimbals have auto-lock and quick-release plates that speed this up. Rebalance after changing lenses or adding a microphone. When walking, bend your knees and roll your feet, pick the follow mode that suits the shot and practise before an important shoot.

## Batteries, Cables and Firmware

Keep the gimbal charged and carry the right control cable for your camera model, since a missing cable can disable features such as start and stop recording. Update firmware when the maker recommends it, because updates often improve camera compatibility. A cheap carry case protects the motors when the gimbal travels in a bag.

## Who Should Skip This

If your camera already has strong in-body stabilisation and you mostly walk slowly, a gimbal adds weight and setup time for modest gains, and a good handheld grip may be enough. Shooters who film quick events with constantly changing lenses will also find that rebalancing after every swap slows them down.

## Gimbal or Stabilised Lens

Some lenses and bodies already have good built-in stabilisation, and a gimbal is mostly needed when you walk, run or move through spaces. For slow pans and tripod shots it adds little. Check how your camera's stabilisation performs while walking in reviews, and decide whether the extra bag weight and setup time are worth it for the footage you shoot.
