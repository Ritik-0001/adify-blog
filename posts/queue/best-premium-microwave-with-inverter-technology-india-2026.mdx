## How an inverter microwave behaves

A conventional microwave controls power by switching the magnetron fully on and off. At 50 percent power, it runs at full blast for a few seconds, then stops, then repeats. That is why edges can overcook while the centre stays cold, and why reheated rice can be scalding in one spot and cool in another.

An inverter microwave uses a power supply that can deliver continuous energy at lower levels. At 50 percent, it truly runs at half power the whole time. The result is gentler, steadier cooking and defrosting with fewer hot and cold spots. It also allows a smaller, lighter power unit.

## Where it makes a real difference

- **Defrosting:** meat, fish and paneer defrost more evenly, with less cooked edge.
- **Reheating rice, dal and curries:** less drying at the edges and fewer cold centres.
- **Delicate foods:** custard, melted chocolate, ghee and milk are less likely to boil over or split.
- **Steaming:** vegetables, idlis and fish retain more texture at controlled power.
- **Cooking at low power:** slow simmering mode for dal or rice can work better.

Where it matters less: boiling a cup of water or quick reheating of a plate at full power. There, an inverter model performs about like a regular one.

## Inverter and convection together

Premium inverter models are commonly convection combos, meaning they also have a fan and heater for baking and a grill for toasting. That makes them useful for cakes, cookies, pizzas and tikkas in addition to reheating. Check the maximum convection temperature, which is generally around 200-220 degrees C, and capacity of 27-32 litres for a family of four.
