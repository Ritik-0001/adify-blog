A wall-mount RO purifier (roughly ₹15,000-25,000) needs four things before the technician arrives: an inlet water tap within reach, a drain point for reject water, a grounded power socket and enough incoming pressure. Get these four right and installation takes under an hour; get them wrong and you will hear about leaks and slow flow for months.

This guide is researched from manufacturer installation manuals and common Indian plumbing practice, not from performing installs ourselves. Before ordering, check whether the kitchen wall is brick or a thin partition board, since drilling for a wall mount behaves very differently on each.

## Pressure: the first thing that goes wrong

An RO membrane needs a minimum inlet pressure, often quoted around 1.5 to 2 bar for the membrane to push water through efficiently. Many Indian apartments with overhead tanks deliver much less, especially on the upper floors. Low pressure produces slow flow, high reject water and a longer tank-filling time.

Most home purifiers include a built-in booster pump that compensates. If your pressure is weak, choose a model with a stronger pump, or add an inline booster. Do not assume the bundled pump will rescue extremely poor pressure. Ask the installer to check flow at the inlet tap before they connect anything.

## Where the water comes from

Take the inlet from a dedicated tap, not from the sink mixer. A plain brass or CP tee on the supply pipe, with its own stop valve, is the cleanest design. The purifier's inlet pipe should be short and free of kinks. Connecting to a hot water line by mistake damages the membrane, so confirm that the tap is cold-only.

If your supply comes from a borewell, put a sediment filter or a spin filter ahead of the purifier. Sand and silt choke a membrane faster than dissolved salts do.

## The drain line and reject water

Every RO sends roughly one to three litres of reject water to the drain for each litre of purified water, depending on the model and your TDS. The drain pipe must run downhill into the sink or a floor drain, with a slight slope and no loops that trap water. Pressure-fit the drain with a clamp so that it does not pop out when the system flushes.

Reject water is useful. Collect it in a bucket for mopping, plants or washing utensils, though not for drinking or cooking. Some households route it to a storage tank for flushing or cleaning. A reject-water saver is sold separately for a modest sum, but check [RO reject water saver kits](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=ro+reject+water+saver+kit&tag=adifystore-21) only if your model supports one.

## Power and placement

A grounded 5 amp socket within about a metre is the usual requirement. Keep the plug high on the wall, away from splashes, and use a socket with a switch so that the purifier can be disconnected when you travel. A [voltage stabilizer for RO](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=voltage+stabilizer+for+water+purifier&tag=adifystore-21) helps only where supply swings are common, since many modern purifiers include their own protection.

Mount the unit at a comfortable tap height, usually near the sink, and leave room above to open the cover and replace the filters. Putting it behind a cabinet door makes service painful.

## Wall mount or countertop

Wall mount is the standard for owners who can drill. On a thin partition wall or tiles that you do not want to drill, a [countertop RO purifier](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=countertop+ro+water+purifier+installation&tag=adifystore-21) avoids drilling but still needs an inlet and drain line. A purifier on a shelf with a hose is not much different in plumbing terms, so plan for the same four elements.

## Installation mistakes that cost money

1. Letting the installer cut a tap off the main supply without a stop valve, so the next service needs a plumber.
2. Leaving the drain pipe higher than the outlet, which causes back-flow into the purifier.
3. Skipping the pressure check on an upper-floor flat.
4. Using a damaged or aged flexible hose as the inlet pipe.
5. Forgetting to flush the first tank. New membranes carry preservative, so the first fill should be thrown away.

## During and after installation

Stay present during installation. Check each pipe clamp with a gentle tug, turn the water on and watch for a drip for ten minutes, and note the TDS before and after the purifier with a handheld meter. Ask the installer to write down the date and the model's serial number for warranty records.

Keep a spare inlet fitting and a few feet of pipe in the kitchen, since leaks usually start at a joint, not at the machine. Buy a [TDS meter](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=tds+meter+digital+water+tester&tag=adifystore-21) to check output periodically, but note that it is a low-cost tool, below this site's usual price floor, mentioned only because it helps you catch membrane decay early.

## Rentals and flats

If your landlord forbids drilling, ask for permission on a tile joint rather than a tile face, or consider a countertop model. Take photos of the wall before and after, since some landlords deduct for holes.

## Recommendation

Spend an hour planning before the installer shows up: locate the inlet tap, measure the slope to the drain, check the socket and test pressure. Choose a model with a booster pump appropriate to your floor, and insist the stop valve and drain clamp are fitted properly. A well-installed RO at roughly ₹15,000-25,000 should run quietly for years, while a poorly installed one fails early.
