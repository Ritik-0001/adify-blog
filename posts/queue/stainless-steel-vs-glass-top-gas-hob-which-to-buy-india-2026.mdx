## Cleaning after tadka and dal spills

Glass has a flat, non-porous surface, so a damp cloth lifts most spills in seconds. Burnt milk and turmeric stains come off with a mild cleaner, though dried sugar syrup can need careful scraping with a plastic scraper rather than a steel one.

Stainless steel hides small drips better but shows water spots, fingerprints and oil film, and baked-on stains need a scouring pad that dulls the finish over time. Brushed steel hides scratches better than mirror-polished steel. If you clean once a day, glass wins; if you clean weekly, steel looks less shabby between wipes.

## Durability and what breaks

Glass is tough against heat but not against impact. A heavy pressure cooker dropped on the corner can crack or shatter it, and a cracked top is usually a replacement, not a repair. Toughened glass also reacts badly to cold water hitting a very hot surface, so never splash it during cooking.

Steel dents and scratches but almost never fails outright. For a rental, a household with young children, or a kitchen where heavy utensils get slid across the top, steel is the safer bet. See [stainless steel 3 burner hob](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=stainless+steel+3+burner+hob&tag=adifystore-21).

## Heat behaviour

Both materials sit around the burners rather than under the pan, so cooking speed is the same; the burner and pan matter, not the top. Steel tops get hot near the burners and discolour to a golden-bronze tint, which some people find ugly. Glass can retain heat longer around the flame and has a small risk of staining from boil-over that bakes on.

Glass hobs usually have a tempered edge that can crack if the wrong pan size overhangs the burner, so use a pan that is not much wider than the burner ring.

## Looks and kitchen design

Black glass pairs well with modern laminates and quartz counters and makes a small kitchen feel tidier. Steel suits traditional layouts and gives a commercial look that matches stainless sinks and chimneys. In an open kitchen, glass is the more common choice.

## Cost and repair

Steel hobs are typically cheaper for the same burner count and brand. Glass models with brass burners and auto-ignition tend to sit slightly higher. A replacement glass panel costs a good fraction of the price of a new hob when it is available at all, so factor that in. The top material is secondary to burner quality. Whatever you choose, check for brass burners, flame failure protection, a sturdy pan support and a removable drip tray. ## Who should pick steel

- Households with heavy cookware and frequent pot drops
- Rented homes where you will move the hob later
- Buyers who prefer a lower price and do not mind water spots
- Kitchens with hard water, which leaves marks on glass

## Who should pick glass

- People who want a smooth, easy-to-wipe finish
- Modern or open kitchens where appearance matters
- Cooks who clean right after each meal

## Who should skip both

If your kitchen counter has no cutout and you cook on a free-standing stove, a built-in hob adds installation cost. A simple gas stove with a glass or steel top may be enough. And if you have an induction cooktop already, a second gas hob only makes sense if you cook large Indian meals that need an open flame.

## Recommendation
