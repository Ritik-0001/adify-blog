## 1. DVR Systems

Roughly ₹15,000–45,000. A DVR (digital video recorder) works with analogue HD cameras over coaxial cable, with separate power supplies for each camera. Parts are simple and cheap, but older systems offer lower resolution and more cables to manage.

## 2. NVR Systems

Roughly ₹25,000–80,000. An NVR (network video recorder) works with IP cameras over network cables and can supply power through PoE, so one cable carries both. You get higher resolution, a cleaner install and easy scaling, at a higher cost.

## 3. Hybrid and HVR Recorders

Roughly ₹15,000–50,000. Hybrid recorders can handle analogue and IP cameras together, which helps if you upgrade gradually. Features depend on the model, so read the supported camera types.

## 4. Image Quality and Features

IP cameras on NVR systems often offer higher resolution, better low-light performance and smart features such as person and vehicle detection. Analogue HD can be good for general use at lower cost, but it costs you detail when you zoom into recorded footage.

## 5. Cabling and Installation

DVR systems use coaxial cable and separate power, while NVR PoE uses one network cable per camera. If you already have coax in place, a DVR upgrade can reuse it, and if you are starting fresh, PoE is simpler and tidier.

## 6. Expansion and Remote Access

NVR systems usually scale more easily and support modern apps, while DVRs can be limited by recorder channels. Both support phone viewing when connected to the internet.

## How to Choose Between DVR and NVR

Start with cabling. If your property already has coax cables, a DVR may save money, and if you are cabling from scratch, an NVR with PoE is neater.

Think about resolution. If you need to read number plates or recognise faces at a distance, IP cameras give better detail.

Consider the future. If you may add cameras or upgrade, an NVR gives more headroom.

## Upgrading an Existing System

If you already have a DVR system, you do not always need to replace everything. Some recorders support higher-resolution analogue cameras on the same cables, and hybrid recorders accept IP cameras too. Ask an installer to test your cabling before you spend, because reusing good cable can save a large part of the cost of a new system.

## Storage, Power and Service

Calculate storage by camera count, resolution and days of retention, and use a surveillance-grade drive. Put the recorder on a UPS in a ventilated, secure spot, and change the default passwords at setup. Ask about warranty on the recorder, cameras and drive, and who services them in your city.

## Who Should Skip This

If you only want to watch one door or a single room, a Wi-Fi camera is cheaper and simpler than either recorder type. Skip a full DVR or NVR kit too if you rent and cannot drill walls or pull cable, since the install cost and effort is wasted when you move out.

## Reading Kit Listings Carefully

Camera count on the box is only half the story. Look at the recorder's channel count, how many cameras are actually included, whether cables and power supplies are in the kit and the size of the hard drive. A cheap 8 camera DVR bundle that ships with short cables and a small drive can cost more once you buy the missing parts than a better kit that includes them.

## Recommendation

For new installs, an NVR with PoE is usually the better long-term choice, and a DVR is sensible when you reuse existing cables on a budget.
