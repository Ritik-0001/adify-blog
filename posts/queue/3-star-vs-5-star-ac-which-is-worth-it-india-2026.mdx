## 1. What the Star Rating Measures

Star ratings are based on an efficiency measure called ISEER, which looks at cooling delivered per unit of electricity across a typical season. More stars mean better seasonal efficiency, and the exact thresholds are set by the energy authority and can change over time. It is a standard way to compare, though real use differs from the test.

Compressor type, condenser coil material, inverter design, installation quality and regular cleaning all affect real-world efficiency, sometimes as much as the star rating. They are harder to compare than a label, which is why the label gets all the attention.

## How to Decide

Estimate your usage. Count the hours per day and months per year you really run the AC, and be honest about shoulder seasons.

Check your tariff. Slab-based electricity rates make heavy use more expensive per unit, which improves the case for 5-star.

Compare real quotes. Look at the live price of the 3-star and 5-star versions of the same model and compute your own payback.

## Habits Versus Stars

A thermostat set at 24 to 26 degrees, the sleep or eco mode, closed curtains and a yearly service can trim a bill by roughly as much as a step up in star rating, and they cost nothing. A 5-star AC left at 18 degrees all night can easily use more than a 3-star unit run sensibly, so the label is a starting point, not a guarantee.

## Who Should Skip This

If your electricity bill is mostly fan, lights and a fridge and the AC runs only on the hottest nights, the star debate is not worth much of your time, and a reliable 3-star inverter with a good warranty will do. People moving house within a couple of years should also skip the 5-star premium, as the payback period may outlast the stay.

## Recommendation

Do the payback calculation with your own usage. For heavy daily use, 5-star is usually worth it, and for light use, a 3-star from a reliable brand is a sensible choice. Confirm the label against the exact model number before you order.
