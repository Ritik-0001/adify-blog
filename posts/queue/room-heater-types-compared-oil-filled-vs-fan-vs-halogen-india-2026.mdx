## How each type actually warms you

An oil-filled radiator heats oil sealed inside metal fins, which warm the surface and then the air by convection. It heats slowly but keeps radiating after the element switches off. A fan heater, usually with a ceramic PTC element, blows air over a hot element and warms a small space quickly. A halogen or quartz heater glows and gives out infrared radiation that warms you directly rather than the air. These differences determine the use case.

## Oil-filled radiator: best for long, quiet warmth

A 13-fin oil-filled heater of 2,500 to 2,900 watts takes around 30 to 45 minutes to warm a bedroom of about 150 square feet, but its thermostat cycles on and off, so average electricity use is lower than the label suggests. It is silent, has no glowing parts, and does not dry the air as aggressively as a fan. Premium models such as De'Longhi's oil-filled range cost roughly ₹15,000 to ₹25,000, with timers and better safety. Weaknesses are weight, slow warm-up and a bulky shape. Ceramic fan heaters warm a bathroom or study within minutes. The trade-offs are noise, dry air and a heater that feels warm only while the fan blows. For a quick morning boost they are excellent. ## Halogen heater: best only for short spot heating

Halogen heaters, typically 800 to 1,200 watts and costing a few thousand rupees, glow orange and warm you within seconds. They suit sitting in front of one, not heating a room. The glowing element is hot, cloth can catch fire if placed too close and many cheaper models lack a tip-over switch. Because they are cheap, they fall below this site's price floor, so consider them only for brief use. Skip them in children's rooms or where elderly people sit close.

## Safety in Indian homes

Electric heaters do not use oxygen the way a bukhari, sigri or charcoal angithi does, but they draw heavy current. A 2.5 kW heater pulls over 10 amps, so use a 16-ampere socket and thick wire, never an extension board shared with other loads. Look for ISI marking, thermal cut-off and a tip-over switch. Never dry clothes on a heater. Keep it at least a metre from curtains. A [heavy-duty extension cord rated 16 amp](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=16+amp+heavy+duty+extension+board&tag=adifystore-21) is the only extension to consider.

## Room size and power

A common thumb rule is roughly 1 kW per 100 square feet in a moderately insulated room, more in drafty homes. A 150 square foot bedroom suits 1.5 to 2.5 kW. A small bathroom needs 1 to 1.5 kW. A large living room of 300 square feet may strain a single oil-filled heater. Sealing gaps with [door draught stoppers](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=door+draught+stopper+winter&tag=adifystore-21) helps more than a bigger heater.

## Running cost and humidity

At roughly ₹7 to ₹9 per unit, a 2.5 kW heater running continuously costs around ₹18 to ₹22 per hour. A thermostat-controlled oil-filled heater cycles, so a night of eight hours might cost ₹70 to ₹120. Fan heaters and halogens run flat out. All electric heaters make air feel drier by lowering relative humidity, though they do not remove moisture. A [room humidifier](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=room+humidifier+ultrasonic+bedroom&tag=adifystore-21) or a bowl of water helps, and drink more water.

## Who should pick which

Pick an oil-filled radiator for bedrooms, children's rooms and elderly users. Pick a fan heater for bathrooms, study corners and quick warm-ups. Pick a halogen only for a short session in a spot you supervise. If your area has severe winters, consider a [hot and cold inverter AC](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=hot+and+cold+inverter+split+AC+1.5+ton&tag=adifystore-21), which delivers heat at much lower running cost.

## Who should skip a room heater

Skip a heater if you live in a mild winter city with only a couple of cold weeks, where a good blanket and a hot water bag do the job. Skip heaters if your wiring is old and thin.
